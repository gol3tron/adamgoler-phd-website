What this answers: How much runway you need to lift off, and to clear a 50 ft obstacle, in a Cessna 172S using the short-field technique (flaps 10°).

How it works

The calculator turns your altimeter setting and field elevation into pressure altitude: PA = elevation + (29.92 − altimeter) × 1000. It then interpolates the POH short-field takeoff chart across weight, temperature, and pressure altitude.

It applies the POH corrections. Headwind: subtract 10% for every 9 knots. Tailwind: add 10% for every 2 knots. Grass: add 15% of the ground roll figure to both distances.

Limits: The chart assumes a paved, level, dry runway, full power, and good technique. It does not cover runway slope or contamination. Outside the chart's range (2200–2550 lb, 0–40 °C, 0–8000 ft PA) the numbers are clamped to the nearest edge, so treat them with suspicion. Real airplanes and real pilots rarely match the book. Add a margin.

Pre-takeoff briefing

What this is: A briefing script built from the numbers above and the 172S POH procedures. It updates as you type. Edit it, then copy or print it.

How it works

It walks through conditions, the takeoff, the abort checkpoint, and what you do if the engine quits on the roll or after liftoff. Procedures and speeds come from POH Sections 3 and 4. The abort checkpoint is the 50-70 rule done right: 70% of liftoff speed by half the book ground roll, after adding a 50% buffer. It turns yellow past a third of the runway and red past half.

What this answers: Your true airspeed and fuel burn at a given altitude, temperature, and RPM in a Cessna 172S.

How it works

The calculator finds pressure altitude from your altimeter setting, then interpolates the POH cruise performance table across pressure altitude, temperature, and RPM.

Limits: The POH table assumes recommended lean mixture, 2550 lb, and wheel fairings. Expect a few knots less without fairings or at a heavier weight. Fuel flow is gallons per hour at that power setting; it does not include taxi, climb, or reserve.
