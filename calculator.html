Climb Gradient Planner

Will I make the departure procedure's climb gradient?

What this answers: Your worst-case climb gradient in feet per nautical mile over a climb, with winds, temperature, and climb rate all changing as you go up. Compare it to the gradient on a departure procedure or obstacle departure.

How it works

Departure procedures publish gradients in ft/nm because obstacles sit at fixed distances. Your airplane climbs in feet per minute, and the ground goes by at groundspeed. So gradient (ft/nm) = climb rate (fpm) ÷ (groundspeed ÷ 60).

The planner slices the climb into 500 ft layers. In each layer it blends your start and end values for climb rate, temperature, wind, and heading. It converts your climb IAS to true airspeed using pressure altitude and temperature, then applies the headwind component to get groundspeed. The layer with the lowest gradient is your worst case, and that's the number that has to beat the chart.

Limits: Garbage in, garbage out. Use climb rates you actually see at your weight, not brochure numbers. Climb rate drops roughly linearly with altitude here, which is close for most piston airplanes. Winds aloft forecasts can be wrong. Tailwinds hurt the most. Leave a buffer.

Climb Gradient Quick Check

Convert between ft/nm and fpm at your groundspeed.

What this answers: What climb rate a published gradient needs at your groundspeed, and whether the climb rate you've got is enough.

How it works

fpm = ft/nm × groundspeed ÷ 60. At 90 knots you cover 1.5 nm a minute, so a 200 ft/nm standard climb needs 300 fpm. The percent figure is ft/nm ÷ 6076 × 100, since a nautical mile is 6,076 ft.

Limits: Use groundspeed, not airspeed. A headwind helps your gradient. A tailwind hurts it. For a climb where things change with altitude, use the planner above.

Time · Speed · Distance

Fill in any two. Get the third.

What this answers: How long a leg takes, how far you'll go, or how fast you need to go.

How it works

distance = groundspeed × time. Leave one box empty and it solves for that one. Type time as minutes (90) or hours and minutes (1:30).

Limits: This uses a single groundspeed for the whole leg. Climb, descent, and changing winds will stretch it.

Fuel Planner

Trip fuel, endurance, and what's left when you land.

What this answers: How much fuel the trip takes, how long and how far you can fly on what's in the tanks, and whether you land with your reserve intact.

How it works

Trip time is distance ÷ groundspeed. Trip fuel is trip time × fuel burn. Endurance is (fuel on board − taxi fuel) ÷ fuel burn, and range is endurance times groundspeed. "Fuel at landing" turns yellow if you dip into your reserve and red if you run out.

Limits: One burn rate for the whole flight. Climb burns more, so add some. Use usable fuel, not total. The FAA minimum reserve under Part 91 VFR is 30 minutes by day and 45 at night. IFR is 45 minutes after reaching your alternate. Most of us carry more.

Wind Components

Headwind and crosswind for a runway.

What this answers: How much of the wind is down the runway and how much is across it.

How it works

Take the angle between the wind and the runway. headwind = wind × cos(angle) and crosswind = wind × sin(angle). Enter the runway's actual heading (270 for runway 27). If the gust factor is set, it also shows the gust crosswind.

Limits: ATIS and ASOS winds are magnetic. METAR and TAF winds are true. Match them to your runway heading, or you can be off by the local variation. Compare the crosswind to your airplane's demonstrated crosswind and to your own limits.

Pressure & Density Altitude

How high the airplane thinks it is.

What this answers: The pressure altitude you'll need for performance charts, and the density altitude your engine and wings will actually feel.

How it works

Pressure altitude: PA = elevation + (29.92 − altimeter) × 1000. Standard temperature at that altitude: ISA = 15 − 2 × PA ÷ 1000 °C. Density altitude uses the common rule of thumb DA ≈ PA + 120 × (OAT − ISA).

Limits: The 120 ft per degree rule is a good approximation, usually within a hundred feet or so of the full calculation at typical field elevations. It ignores humidity, which adds a bit more on muggy days. Most POH charts want pressure altitude and temperature, not density altitude.
